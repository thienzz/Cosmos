# Cosmos Explorer: User Personas

**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** 2026-04-16  
**Status:** Final  
**Product:** Cosmos Explorer (Interactive 3D Web-Based Universe Visualization)

---

## Executive Summary

This document defines six detailed user personas for Cosmos Explorer, representing the primary target audiences and use cases. Each persona includes demographic information, goals, pain points, technical proficiency, devices, key scenarios, and success criteria. The personas inform feature prioritization, UX decisions, and marketing strategy.

---

## Persona 1: The Space Dreamer

**Primary Persona — Highest Priority**

### Profile
- **Name:** Marcus Chen
- **Age:** 22 years old
- **Location:** Portland, Oregon, USA
- **Occupation:** College sophomore, working part-time at a coffee shop
- **Photo Description:** Enthusiastic young man wearing a NASA hoodie, sitting at home with a second monitor showing a SpaceX livestream; casual but engaged expression

### Demographics & Background
Marcus is a college student majoring in computer science with a passion for space exploration that started at age 6 watching documentaries. He watches every SpaceX Starship test and NASA launch livestream, follows space YouTubers like Kurzgesagt and Veritasium, and has a modest collection of space merchandise. He grew up in a suburban home and has access to a mid-range laptop (2-3 years old, 8GB RAM). He spends 15+ hours per week consuming space content and dreams of traveling to space someday.

### Goals
**Primary:**
- Experience a visceral sense of the universe's scale and beauty in an immersive 3D environment
- "Fly through" the cosmos and feel the vastness of space in a way that awe-inspires him
- Explore planetary orbits, star systems, and galaxy clusters in real-time
- Share his discoveries with friends and on social media to engage others in space passion

**Secondary:**
- Learn the names and locations of real celestial objects
- Understand orbital mechanics through interactive visualization
- Create shareable content (screenshots, short clips) to inspire others

### Frustrations & Pain Points
- Existing planetarium software feels outdated and unintuitive
- Static 2D diagrams in textbooks don't convey the true scale of space
- High-end professional astronomy software is too technical and expensive
- Wants stunning visuals but current tools require advanced knowledge
- Limited ability to explore at his own pace without expert guidance
- Can't use tools on his aging laptop without lag or crashes

### Technical Proficiency
**4 out of 5** — Comfortable with software, games, and technical interfaces; quick learner; can troubleshoot basic issues; doesn't need hand-holding but appreciates smooth onboarding.

### Devices Used
- Primary: Mid-range laptop (Windows or Mac, 8GB RAM, Intel i5/i7 or equivalent)
- Secondary: iPhone 12 or similar (browsing, casual exploration)
- Occasional: Friend's gaming desktop (for better performance sessions)

### Key Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Late Night Discovery**
Marcus opens Cosmos Explorer on his laptop at 11 PM. He selects "Flyby Mode" and navigates to the Orion Nebula. He "flies" through the nebula at slow speed, taking screenshots at beautiful angles. He spends 45 minutes exploring, then posts his best screenshot to Instagram with the caption "This is the Orion Nebula in 3D. Absolutely mind-blowing."

**Scenario 2: Sharing with Friends**
Marcus is FaceTiming with a college friend who doesn't know much about space. He describes what he's found in Cosmos Explorer, then sends her a link. She opens it, he guides her through navigating to Saturn's rings. They spend 20 minutes exploring together virtually.

**Scenario 3: Pre-Launch Excitement**
Before SpaceX's Starship launch on Thursday, Marcus explores the Cosmos Explorer launch site (Earth view) and then zooms out to see the orbital paths of active satellites. He visualizes the trajectory the rocket will take into orbit. He returns every day for the 3 days leading up to launch.

**Scenario 4: Deep Dive into Scale**
Marcus uses the "Scale Comparison" feature to compare Earth to Jupiter, then Jupiter to the Sun, then the Sun to Betelgeuse. The zoom-out effect gives him chills. He rewatches this sequence multiple times and shows it to his physics class during a group project.

### Representative Quote
*"I want to feel what it's like to actually BE in space, even if it's just on my screen. Not reading about it or watching someone explain it—actually EXPERIENCING the scale and the beauty."*

### Feature Priorities
1. Stunning 3D rendering that works on mid-range hardware
2. Smooth, intuitive camera controls (mouse/keyboard navigation)
3. Ability to travel to any object at any speed
4. Screenshot and video clip capture with easy sharing
5. Real celestial data (accurate positions, names, basic information)
6. Occasional "guided tours" or "highlight" features to discover cool things

### Success Criteria
- Marcus opens Cosmos Explorer at least 2 times per week
- He shares screenshots or clips with friends at least once per month
- His laptop doesn't lag or crash during typical 30-60 minute sessions
- He discovers new objects or features unprompted and feels excited about them
- He refers 2+ friends to Cosmos Explorer within the first month of use
- He spends 8+ hours total in the first month, with session length averaging 20-30 minutes

## Persona 2: The Astronomy Educator

**Primary Persona — High Priority**

### Profile
- **Name:** Dr. Sarah Williams
- **Age:** 42 years old
- **Location:** Austin, Texas, USA
- **Occupation:** High school AP Astronomy & Physics teacher
- **Photo Description:** Engaged woman in professional casual attire standing in front of a classroom with a projector screen showing a partial sky view; thoughtful, approachable expression

### Demographics & Background
Sarah has taught high school astronomy and physics for 15 years at a well-funded STEM-focused school. She holds a Bachelor's in Physics Education and regularly attends professional development in STEM teaching. She is passionate about making astronomy accessible and exciting for her students, many of whom are unengaged with traditional textbooks. Her classroom has a projector, smartboard, and student laptops. She follows NSTA (National Science Teachers Association) resources and is always looking for tools that make complex concepts tangible. She has a 6-year-old child and limited personal time outside of lesson planning.

### Goals
**Primary:**
- Demonstrate orbital mechanics, planetary motion, and stellar scales in a way students can viscerally understand
- Reduce classroom time spent explaining concepts and increase hands-on/interactive exploration
- Show the "why" behind astronomical phenomena (seasons, tides, planetary retrograde motion)
- Engage underperforming students by making the content immersive and visually stunning

**Secondary:**
- Generate lesson plans and classroom activities centered on the tool
- Measure student learning outcomes and engagement improvements
- Create shareable resources for other teachers in her district
- Reduce her prep time by using the tool as the primary teaching medium for certain units

### Frustrations & Pain Points
- Planetarium software is expensive, requires dedicated hardware, and isn't web-accessible
- Textbook diagrams are static and don't help students build mental 3D models
- YouTube videos are passive; students need to INTERACT to learn
- Current tools don't let her pause, annotate, or highlight key features during classroom presentation
- Setting up demonstrations is time-consuming; she needs something plug-and-play
- School IT department is slow to approve new software; web-based solutions are preferred
- Student devices vary widely (Chromebooks, Windows, Macs); tool must work on all
- Limited budget allocation; expensive tools aren't an option

### Technical Proficiency
**3 out of 5** — Comfortable with Google Suite, Zoom, and LMS platforms; can troubleshoot basic issues; needs clear documentation and support but can learn new tools; not a developer.

### Devices Used
- Primary: Classroom projector connected to laptop (Windows, 4-year-old school-issued machine)
- Secondary: Student laptops in classroom (mix of Chromebooks, Windows, Mac)
- Tertiary: Personal laptop at home for planning (Mac)
- Supporting: School smartboard for annotations

### Key Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Demonstrating Orbital Mechanics**
Sarah launches Cosmos Explorer on the classroom projector while students have the same view on their laptops. She shows Earth's orbit around the Sun. Then, she speeds up time on the visualization—students see the orbit complete in 5 seconds. She pauses and asks, "Why do we have seasons?" Students discuss while looking at Earth's axial tilt. She uses the tool's "annotation mode" to draw lines showing the angle of incoming sunlight. Students engage in active discussion. The class collectively discovers why Northern Hemisphere winter occurs.

**Scenario 2: Scaling Exercise**
For a lesson on cosmic scale, Sarah uses Cosmos Explorer's comparison feature. She starts with Earth, then zooms to show Jupiter (10x larger). Then the Sun (100x Jupiter). Then Betelgeuse (1000x the Sun). Students collectively gasp at the Betelgeuse comparison. She then asks them to estimate how many Earths would fit inside Betelgeuse. The visualization makes the abstract concept concrete.

**Scenario 3: Homework Assignment**
Sarah assigns: "Explore three stellar nebulae in Cosmos Explorer and take screenshots. For each, write 100 words on what you observe and what you learned." Students spend 45 minutes exploring on home devices. The tool's accessibility on all platforms means 100% of students can complete the homework.

**Scenario 4: Professional Development Workshop**
Sarah presents Cosmos Explorer at her school's summer STEM workshop for teachers. She demonstrates how to use it in a chemistry class (atomic scale), earth science class (plate tectonics), and physics class (gravity simulation). Other teachers are impressed and request implementation support.

### Representative Quote
*"My students zone out when I lecture about the solar system. But when they can see it in 3D, when they can explore it themselves—that's when they actually GET it. That's when their eyes light up."*

### Feature Priorities
1. Works seamlessly on classroom projector and student Chromebooks
2. Pause/resume and speed controls for demonstrations
3. Annotation tools to highlight features during presentation
4. Lesson plan templates and educational content alignment
5. Ability to lock student views to teacher's view (classroom synchronization)
6. Data accuracy (real orbital parameters, real stellar data)
7. Offline mode or fast-loading cached data (school WiFi can be slow)

### Success Criteria
- Sarah creates and deploys 2+ lessons per semester using Cosmos Explorer
- Student engagement metrics improve by 20%+ on astronomy units compared to prior years
- At least 3 other teachers in her school adopt the tool for their classes
- She integrates the tool into her standard curriculum by end of academic year
- Students show improved conceptual understanding on pre/post tests
- She receives positive parent feedback about student enthusiasm for space topics
- Sarah recommends the tool to 5+ teacher colleagues at conferences or professional groups

## Persona 5: The Amateur Astronomer

**Secondary Persona — Important for Feature Depth**

### Profile
- **Name:** Robert "Bob" Patterson
- **Age:** 58 years old
- **Location:** Asheville, North Carolina, USA
- **Occupation:** Retired software engineer, now pursuing astronomy hobby
- **Photo Description:** Experienced-looking man in casual outdoor clothing standing next to a telescope under a starry sky; contemplative, passionate expression

### Demographics & Background
Bob retired 3 years ago and has rekindled a childhood passion for astronomy. He owns a good quality telescope (8-inch reflector) and has spent time learning to identify constellations, bright stars, and deep-sky objects. He is part of a local astronomy club that meets monthly. He has a strong technical background (was a software engineer) but has been out of day-to-day programming for 5 years. He spends most clear nights observing from his backyard and keeps a notebook of observations. He is knowledgeable about celestial coordinates, magnitude, and deep-sky object catalogs (Messier, NGC). He is thoughtful, careful, and prefers accuracy over flashiness. He uses a desktop computer and is comfortable with technical interfaces.

### Goals
**Primary:**
- Cross-reference his telescope observations with a 3D model to understand spatial relationships
- Plan observing sessions (identify what's visible tonight, where in the sky, optimal viewing times)
- Learn the 3D structure of constellations and understand why they look the way they do
- Identify and explore deep-sky objects from the Messier and NGC catalogs

**Secondary:**
- Share his observing experiences with his astronomy club using the tool
- Track which objects he's observed and which he still wants to see
- Understand the physical properties of objects he observes (distance, size, composition)
- Create custom observing lists based on difficulty, brightness, or object type

### Frustrations & Pain Points
- Printed star charts are 2D and don't help him understand 3D spatial relationships
- Planetarium software (Stellarium, Cartes du Ciel) is complex and feels over-engineered for casual use
- Doesn't know the 3D positions of galaxies and nebulae; they seem random in the sky
- Current tools don't let him identify what he's looking at through his eyepiece in real-time
- Would like to compare his visual observations with actual 3D structure of objects
- Concerns about accuracy; wants data from authoritative sources (Hipparcos, Gaia, etc.)
- Wants a tool that feels like it's designed for observers, not just casual browsers

### Technical Proficiency
**3.5 out of 5** — Strong technical background but somewhat out of practice; comfortable with complex software but prefers intuitive design; appreciates accuracy and detail; can troubleshoot issues independently.

### Devices Used
- Primary: Desktop PC with large monitor (Windows or Linux, 6 years old but well-maintained)
- Secondary: Laptop for planning sessions at the telescope (basic specs)
- Supporting: Smartphone (basic phone for setting timers during observation)

### Key Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Observing Plan**
Bob is preparing for a night of observing. He opens Cosmos Explorer and inputs his location (Asheville, NC) and observation date/time (tonight, 9 PM). The tool shows him which constellations and bright objects are visible tonight from his location. He decides to observe the Orion Nebula and uses the tool to see its 3D position and size. He notes the expected angular size through his telescope. He also sees that the Pleiades are visible tonight and plans to compare the visual appearance to the 3D model.

**Scenario 2: Understanding What He Sees**
Bob is observing M51 (Whirlpool Galaxy) through his telescope. He can see the spiral structure and a companion galaxy nearby. Later, he opens Cosmos Explorer and navigates to M51. The 3D view shows him the actual structure and reveals why the companion galaxy appears so close in the sky (it's actually at a different distance, but aligned from Earth's perspective). He has an "aha!" moment about the projection effect of viewing 3D structures from a 2D perspective.

**Scenario 3: Club Presentation**
Bob is giving a presentation at his astronomy club about "The Orion Region." He uses Cosmos Explorer on the club's projector to show the 3D layout of stars and nebulae in Orion. He zooms to show how the nebulae are scattered throughout 3D space, not all at the same distance. This helps members understand why the Orion Nebula and Reflection Nebula are different types of objects even though they're in the same constellation. Members are impressed and ask questions about how to access the tool.

**Scenario 4: Deep-Dive Exploration**
Bob is cataloging his observations for the year. He has observed 47 Messier objects so far. He opens Cosmos Explorer and searches for "Messier catalog objects visible from Asheville in April." The tool shows a filtered list. He explores each one in 3D, checking off which ones he's observed and noting which ones he wants to revisit or attempt. He exports his observing log and notes to share with his club.

### Representative Quote
*"I want to understand the geometry of what I'm seeing through my eyepiece. A 2D picture doesn't cut it—I need to see the real 3D relationships to fully appreciate what's out there."*

### Feature Priorities
1. Accurate data from authoritative sources (Gaia, Hipparcos, Messier, NGC catalogs)
2. Ability to filter and search by catalog (Messier, NGC, star brightness, constellation)
3. Observability information (altitude, azimuth from a given location and time)
4. Distance and size information for objects
5. 3D spatial visualization showing how objects are actually distributed in space
6. Integration with personal observation logs
7. Exporting capabilities for notes and data

### Success Criteria
- Bob uses Cosmos Explorer at least 1x per week for observing planning or post-observation learning
- He incorporates Cosmos Explorer into at least 2 club presentations or discussions
- He explores at least 60 Messier objects over 6 months
- He reports that his understanding of constellation geometry has improved
- He refers the tool to at least 5 club members
- He uses the tool for at least 1 observing session per month (active integration into hobby)
- He feels confident using the tool for identification and planning without significant learning curve

## Persona 6: The Researcher

**Secondary Persona — Important for Feature Credibility**

### Profile
- **Name:** Dr. Priya Kapoor
- **Age:** 29 years old
- **Location:** Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA
- **Occupation:** PhD student in Astrophysics at Harvard University
- **Photo Description:** Focused young woman in casual academic attire working at a desk with multiple monitors showing data tables and research papers; intelligent, driven expression

### Demographics & Background
Priya is a 4th year PhD student studying galaxy clusters and large-scale structure of the universe. She works with cosmological simulations and observational data from surveys like SDSS and 2dF. Her research involves analyzing spatial distributions of galaxies, redshift distributions, and structure formation. She is comfortable with statistical analysis, data visualization, and programming (Python, IDL). She works on a university-provided workstation with dual monitors and plenty of computational resources. She is under time pressure (papers to write, degree to finish) and values tools that save time and improve clarity of presentation. She is published in peer-reviewed journals and understands the importance of accuracy and proper data sourcing.

### Goals
**Primary:**
- Quickly visualize large catalogs of survey data to check spatial distributions and spot anomalies
- Create publication-quality figures for papers showing 3D structure of galaxy clusters and filaments
- Cross-check observational catalog positions with simulations to understand structure
- Communicate research findings to collaborators and at conferences using compelling 3D visualizations

**Secondary:**
- Verify data accuracy and identify outliers or errors in large catalogs
- Explore redshift-dependent structures and the evolution of large-scale structure
- Generate 3D coordinates and distance information for specific catalogs (for research papers)
- Use high-resolution visualizations in presentations and posters

### Frustrations & Pain Points
- Writing custom Python scripts to visualize 3D data is time-consuming; most plotting libraries are 2D
- Creating publication-quality 3D figures requires expensive software (IDL, MATLAB) or learning specialized tools
- Catalog data is often in different formats (FITS, ASCII, SQL databases); no unified access
- Need to verify that visualized data matches actual catalog values exactly
- Can't easily share interactive 3D visualizations with collaborators remotely
- Professional 3D visualization software has steep learning curves
- Time pressure means she needs tools that work immediately, not require weeks of setup
- Wants to cite tools used in visualizations; needs clear documentation of data sources

### Technical Proficiency
**4.5 out of 5** — Highly technical; comfortable with Python, data analysis, statistical software, and command-line tools; learns new software quickly; understands data structures and formats; can troubleshoot independently.

### Devices Used
- Primary: University workstation (Linux, dual monitors, high-end specs for research)
- Secondary: Laptop for mobile work and remote collaboration (Mac or Linux)
- Supporting: Server/computing cluster for large simulations and data processing

### Key Scenarios

**Scenario 1: Catalog Visualization**
Priya has a new catalog of galaxy cluster members (500 objects) from her latest survey. She exports the data as a CSV with RA, Dec, and redshift. She uploads this to Cosmos Explorer's "Custom Catalog" feature. The tool renders the 3D positions of the galaxies, color-coded by redshift. She immediately spots a few objects that seem misplaced and flags them for re-inspection. She identifies a new filament structure connecting two clusters. She exports a high-resolution PNG of the best angle for her paper's figures section.

**Scenario 2: Conference Presentation**
Priya is presenting her research at the American Astronomical Society meeting. Instead of static 2D plots, she uses Cosmos Explorer on the projector to show an interactive 3D visualization of her galaxy cluster results. She rotates the view, zooms in on substructures, and shows the redshift distribution using color. The audience is engaged. After the talk, three researchers ask for the link to explore her data themselves.

**Scenario 3: Collaboration & Feedback**
Priya shares a Cosmos Explorer link with her PhD advisor showing the 3D structure of her simulated galaxy cluster. The advisor can rotate and zoom to examine details. They discuss specific features visible in 3D that weren't apparent in 2D plots. The collaborative discussion is more efficient because both parties are viewing the same interactive visualization. The advisor suggests angles to investigate further. Priya marks those angles as "observations" in the tool.

**Scenario 4: Data Verification**
Before submitting her paper, Priya uses Cosmos Explorer to triple-check that her catalog's spatial distribution matches her claims in the paper. She imports her catalog and manually verifies a dozen randomly selected objects: Do the coordinates match? Is the 3D structure continuous as she claims? Are there any obvious errors? ## Anti-Personas: Who Cosmos Explorer is NOT For

### 1. The Professional Astronomer Demanding Publication-Level Simulations
- **Who:** PhD-level researchers in computational astrophysics who run custom N-body simulations
- **Why Not Served:** Cosmos Explorer shows real-world catalog data, not simulations. Professional research requires custom code and full computational control. Cosmos Explorer is too generalist for cutting-edge research workflows.
- **Better Alternative:** Astrophysics simulation software (GADGET, GIZMO), custom Python pipelines

### 2. The Mobile-Only User Without Desktop/Laptop Access
- **Who:** People who only have access to phones and no other devices
- **Why Not Served:** While we support mobile, the core experience (3D exploration, navigation) is optimized for larger screens. Mobile is secondary.
- **Better Alternative:** Mobile-native space apps (Stellarium Mobile, SkySafari for phones)

### 3. The Non-Technical User Requiring Complete Hand-Holding
- **Who:** People with very limited computer literacy who need step-by-step voice guidance
- **Why Not Served:** Cosmos Explorer assumes basic digital literacy (clicking, dragging, pinching). While we design for accessibility, we don't provide voice guidance or extreme simplification.
- **Better Alternative:** Physical planetariums, guided museum experiences

### 4. The Conspiracy Theorist / Flat-Earth Advocate
- **Who:** People seeking to use the tool to argue against established astronomy
- **Why Not Served:** Cosmos Explorer displays scientifically accurate data. We don't support alternate "theories" or unsupported claims.
- **Better Alternative:** Not applicable; Cosmos Explorer is not designed for this use case

### 5. The Enterprise Software Buyer (For Institutional Use Without Customization)
- **Who:** Large corporations or institutions needing white-labeled, heavily customized software
- **Why Not Served:** Cosmos Explorer is a consumer/educational product. Enterprise licensing, custom integrations, and dedicated support are out of scope for MVP.
- **Better Alternative:** Enterprise planetarium software (Digital Sky, Space Engine Enterprise)

### 6. The Hardcore Gamer Seeking Gaming-Level Graphics
- **Who:** Players expecting AAA game-quality graphics and physics engine
- **Why Not Served:** Cosmos Explorer prioritizes accuracy and accessibility over cutting-edge graphics. It's an educational tool, not a game engine.
- **Better Alternative:** Space exploration games (Kerbal Space Program, Space Engine, Elite Dangerous)

## Implementation Guidance

### For Product Managers
- Use these personas in roadmap prioritization: Tier 1 features enable Tier 1 personas
- Test new features with 2-3 representatives from primary personas before launch
- Measure success metrics specific to each persona's goals
- Revisit personas annually and update based on real user data

### For Designers
- Optimize UI/UX for the "Casual Explorer" use case (gestures, simplicity) while supporting advanced features for "Researcher"
- Create multiple entry points: guided tours for Space Dreamer, rapid access for Researcher, structured lessons for Educator
- Support both immersive (dark mode, full-screen) and informational (annotations, details) experiences

### For Marketing & Growth
- Primary messaging targets Tier 1 personas (dreamers, educators, creators)
- SEO & content marketing should capture "how to teach orbital mechanics," "beautiful space visualizations," etc.
- Partner with educational institutions and science content creators early
- Encourage user-generated content (screenshots, tutorials) from Space Dreamers

### For Engineering
- Mobile support is important for Casual Explorer but not primary (responsive design, not native app)
- Data accuracy and citation are critical for Researcher credibility
- Performance on mid-range devices (Space Dreamer's laptop) is essential; target 60 FPS on integrated graphics
- Tablet support (iPad) is important for Casual Explorer
